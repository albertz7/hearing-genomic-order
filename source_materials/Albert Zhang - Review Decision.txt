Reviewer Comments 
 
Mapping Mutations to Music: Sonification and Comparative Analysis of Xylella fastidiosa Genomic 
Data 
 
Summary: The manuscript investigates whether sonification can help distinguish sequence differences 
among Xylella fastidiosa  samples by turning sequence data into sound. The study uses three NCBI 
sequences, including one described as a minimally mutated reference a nd two described as disease -
associated strains. The authors pre -process the sequences, transcribe them into mRNA and map 
nucleotides and codons to musical features. They then compare the audio outputs by pitch, tone, 
rhythm and pattern. The authors also us e BLASTp, spectrogram analysis and repeated sonification 
runs as forms of validation. Based on these comparisons, the manuscript states that samples with more 
mutations show more disrupted sound patterns and suggests that sonification may complement 
standard genomic analysis. 
 
Major Issues: 
 
1. The ma nuscript states the general aim  but the aim and hypothesis are not presented clearly 
enough in the introduction. The main idea is present, but it is brief and not framed in a direct, 
testable way. A clearer statement of the study aim and hypothesis would improve the focus of 
the paper.  
2. There is a major biological error in the wording. Xylella fastidiosa is described as “viral” and as 
having a “viral genome,” but it is a bacterial plant pathogen. This should be corrected throughout 
the manuscript. Specifically, the abstract states ‘the v iral pathogens Olive Quick Decline 
Syndrome and Citrus Variegated Chlorosis’ and claims ‘the viral genomes’ showed audio shifts, 
but OQDS and CVC are bacterial diseases, not viral pathogens, and Xylella fastidiosa has no viral 
genome. Describing a bacteriu m as a virus is a fundamental scientific error. That ’s not okay. 
Rewrite the biological framing to correctly identify the organisms as bacterial, and specify the 
exact strain, subspecies, and accession number for each sequence analyzed. 
3. The abstract gives the general topic and the main conclusion but it is still too vague on the method. 
It mentions pitch assignment and validation  but it does not explain the mapping logic or the 
comparison design clearly enough. The abstract should describe the method more directly.  
4. The strain claims do not appear to match the references used to support them. The manuscript 
links the selected viru lent strains to Olive Quick Decline Syndrome and Citrus Variegated 
Chlorosis through references 2 and 3 but these references do not clearly support that use in their 
current form. The disease associations and the supporting citations should be checked care fully 
and replaced with more direct sources if needed. Replace both with primary sources that directly 
document the OQDS and CVC strains chosen for this study, and provide accession numbers for 
all three sequences in the main text. 
5. The study analyzes only three genomic sequences (one reference, two virulent strains). This 
sample size is inadequate for drawing meaningful conclusions about sonification effectiveness. 
Please include minimum 10-15 sequences per strain type to establish statistical significance. 
6. The sequence selection needs very careful verification. The manuscript states that complete 
genomes were selected, but DQ991169.1 and PQ550638.1 do not appear to fit that description as 
presented here. This is a critical issue because the study’s control and comparison set depend on 
those sequence identities being correct.  
7. The description of DQ991169.1 as a “minimally mutated reference genome ” is especially 
concerning. The manuscript treats  it as a genome -level reference but that accession should be 
verified carefully before any comparison is built on it  (they are short pieces of DNA sequences 
not the genomes). If the accession is not the correct reference type, the whole analysis becomes

difficult to interpret. In addition, the term ‘minimally mutated’ is never defined (relative to what 
reference sequence, by what mutation count, and over what genomic region? ). Without a 
definition, the control baseline is undefined and every comparative claim in the results is 
unanchored. 
8. The literature review is too limi ted for a paper built around sonification. Important studies on 
DNA and protein sequence sonification appear to be missing. In particular, Temple (doi: 
10.1186/s12859-017-1632-x), Martin et al. (doi: 10.1186/s12859 -021-04362-7) and Plaisier et al. 
(doi: 10.1186/s13104-021-05685-7) would help place the work in the correct research context and 
clarify what is new here. You are not the first person to research genomic sonification. Please do 
not claim novelty without acknowledging what prior work has done and clearly stating what is 
new in this study specifically. 
9. The manuscript states that sonification can work as an alternative to visual analysis, but it does 
not explain this value strongly enough. The paper should say more clearly why sound would 
help in this setting and what it offers beyond standard sequence comparison tools. At present, 
that justification is still too general.  
10. The pre -processing steps are described too broadly for reproducibility. The authors mention 
removing ambiguous nucleotides, fixing formatting and aligning sequences, but they do not 
name the tools, parameters or decision rules used. These details are needed because they affect 
the sound output directly. 
11. The sonification procedure also needs more detail. The manuscr ipt states that bases and codons 
were mapped to pitches and other sound features, but it does not explain the mapping scheme 
clearly enough for another researcher to repeat the method. The paper should state exactly how 
sequence elements were converted into sound and why those choices were made. The following 
are currently missing and must be provided: the complete pitch -to-nucleotide and codon -to-
sound mapping tables, frequencies assigned to each base and codon, note durations, octave 
ranges, normalization rules, tempo, segmentation window size, all software versions used, and 
the full code or repository link. Without these, there is no way to verify that the claimed audio 
differences reflect sequence differences rather than arbitrary mapping choices. 
12. The reason for transcribing DNA into mRNA before sonification is not explained clearly enough. 
The authors state that this step is biologically meaningful, but they do not show how it improves 
mutation detection in this design. That logic should be stated more directly.  
13. The validation section contains a serious wording problem and a broader method problem. The 
manuscript states that BLASTp was used to compare the sonified seque nces to known protein 
databases but BLASTp compares protein sequences, not audio outp uts. This wording is unclear 
and may be incorrect. More broadly, the validation remains mostly qualitative and needs clearer 
explanation and stronger support. The manuscript claims BLASTp ‘reaffirmed’ that tonal 
variations ‘truly reflected biologically sig nificant mutations,’ but there are no mutation tables, 
aligned loci, E-values, percent identities, or before/after alignments linking any specific sound 
event to any validated sequence change. BLASTp functions as a separate sequence-analysis step 
here. It does not validate the audio. A genuine validation requires a ground -truth benchmark: a 
set of known mutations, predefined audio features that should detect them, and measurable 
sensitivity and specificity. 
14. The paper lacks proper negative controls. Please i nclude sonification of randomly generated 
sequences and sequences from non -pathogenic bacteria to demonstrate that observed patterns 
are specific to pathogenic mutations rather than general sequence variation. 
15. The results are described mostly with broad ph rases such as shifts, breaks and chaotic tonal 
clusters. These claims may be interesting  but they are not supported by figures, tables or 
numerical summaries that let the reader judge the strength of the comparison. This is especially

important because sp ectrogram analysis is mentioned  yet the results are not shown in a clear 
visual or quantitative form. Repeated reruns of the same input only confirm the algorithm is 
deterministic. They do not demonstrate biological validity or mutation -detection accuracy. A 
meaningful results section requires quantitative acoustic features (e.g., spectral centroid, tempo, 
pitch distribution), blinded classification experiments, and comparison against a null model such 
as shuffled-sequence sonification. 
16. There are no statistical tests performed to validate observed differences. Include appropriate tests 
(t-tests, ANOVA) comparing acoustic parameters between strain types. 
17. The discussion and conclusion go beyond the evidence presented. The study uses only three 
sequences, yet the manuscript moves quickly to broader claims about large genomic datasets, 
accessibility and wider biomedical use. These points may be worth mentioning but they should 
be framed more cautiously and balanced with a clearer discussion of limitations.  Do not 
overclaim or overgeneralize. Just report what you found. 
18. The current  reference list contains only 5 citations. The author should expand the review of 
literature with at -least 20 -25 primary research studies including sonification literature, 
bioinformatics methods, and Xylella fastidiosa research. 
 
Minor Issues: 
 
1. The abbreviations OQDS and CVC should be defined clearly at first use in the main text. 
2. There are clear grammar problems in the Abstract and Results sections. e.g., “the viral genomes 
noticeable shifts in tone and pitch” is not grammatically correct.  
3. Some ideas are repeated too often. e.g., the claims about accessibility and pattern recognit ion 
appear in similar form in the abstract, discussion and conclusion.  
4. A short table listing the sequences, accession numbers and disease associations would help the 
reader follow the study more easily. This is especially useful because DQ991169.1 and 
PQ550638.1 are named directly, while the CVC-associated sequence is described less clearly.  
5. The naming of strains, sequences and comparison groups is not consistent enough. e.g., the 
manuscript moves between “least mutated genome, ” “minimally mutated genome, ” “viral 
genome” and “virulent strains,” which makes the comparison harder to follow.  
6. Some terms are too broad and should be made more specific. e.g., “computer simulation” in the 
introduction and “interactive model” in the methods section do not tell the reader enough.  
7. The mention of Redux Slice is too brief for a broad readership. e.g., the methods state that 
playback contr ol was provided by Redux Slice but its role in the workflow is not explained 
clearly enough.
